## How easily something converts to cash without moving its price

Liquidity describes how quickly and easily an asset can be bought or sold
at a stable, fair price. Cash is the most liquid asset of all, by
definition. A share of a large, heavily-traded company is highly liquid --
you can typically buy or sell it almost instantly near the current quoted
price. A stake in a small private business, or a piece of real estate, is
far less liquid -- it can take significant time and effort to find a buyer
at a fair price.

## Why liquidity matters for everyday investors

Highly liquid investments (like large index ETFs) let you enter and exit
positions without the trade itself meaningfully moving the price against
you. Thinly traded, less liquid stocks can have wider bid-ask spreads
(covered in a separate article) and can be harder to sell quickly at a
price close to the last quoted one, especially in a large quantity.

## Liquidity risk during stress

Liquidity can dry up during periods of market stress -- exactly when
investors most want to be able to sell easily. This is one more reason an
emergency fund is generally recommended to sit in genuinely liquid places
like a savings account, rather than in investments that could become harder
to sell quickly at a fair price right when the money is needed.

## Fund-level liquidity vs. holdings-level liquidity

An ETF trades continuously throughout the day, but that doesn't
automatically mean every one of its underlying holdings is highly liquid --
a fund holding less liquid underlying assets can behave differently during
stressed markets than its normal day-to-day trading might suggest.

## Going deeper

For a long-term, buy-and-hold investor in broad index funds, liquidity is
rarely a pressing daily concern -- it becomes more relevant for anyone
trading frequently, trading less common securities, or needing to access
money on short notice.
