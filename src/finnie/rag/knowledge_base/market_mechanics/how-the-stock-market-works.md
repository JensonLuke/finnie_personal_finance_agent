## Exchanges are matchmakers

A stock exchange (like the NYSE or Nasdaq) doesn't set prices itself -- it's
a marketplace that matches buyers and sellers. When you place an order,
it's routed through your broker to an exchange, where it's matched against
someone willing to take the other side of the trade.

## What actually moves a price

A stock's price is simply the most recent price buyers and sellers agreed
on. If more people want to buy at a given price than want to sell at it,
the price tends to rise until enough sellers appear; if more want to sell
than buy, it tends to fall until buyers step back in. This constant
back-and-forth is why prices move throughout the trading day.

## Market indexes are a summary, not a separate investment

An index like the S&P 500 isn't something you can buy directly -- it's a
formula that tracks the combined performance of a specific group of stocks,
used as a benchmark for "how the market is doing." You gain exposure to an
index's performance through a fund that's built to track it.

## Trading hours and after-hours activity

Major US exchanges are open for regular trading roughly 9:30am-4:00pm
Eastern on weekdays, excluding market holidays. Trading also happens outside
those hours ("after-hours" and "pre-market"), but typically with lower
volume and wider price swings, since fewer participants are active.

## Going deeper

Market-wide moves are often driven by economic news (interest rate
decisions, inflation reports, employment data) that shifts investor
expectations broadly, on top of company-specific news that moves individual
stocks. Both layers are happening simultaneously, which is part of why
markets can seem to move for reasons unrelated to any single company's
performance.
