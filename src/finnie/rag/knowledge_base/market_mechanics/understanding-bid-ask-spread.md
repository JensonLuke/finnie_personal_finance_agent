## Two prices, not one

At any given moment, a stock or ETF actually has two quoted prices: the
**bid** (the highest price a buyer is currently willing to pay) and the
**ask** (the lowest price a seller is currently willing to accept). The
difference between them is the "spread." The single "price" you usually see
quoted is typically the last price at which a trade actually happened
between a buyer and seller meeting in the middle.

## Why the spread exists

Market makers and other liquidity providers profit from this small gap in
exchange for standing ready to buy or sell at any moment. In a highly
liquid security with lots of trading activity, competition keeps this
spread very narrow -- often just a cent or a fraction of a cent for large
ETFs. In a thinly traded security, the spread can be much wider.

## Why the spread is a real, if small, cost

If you buy at the ask and immediately turned around to sell, you'd sell at
the (lower) bid -- meaning you'd take a small loss purely from the spread,
with no price movement involved at all. For occasional long-term investors
buying widely-traded funds, this cost is typically negligible. It matters
more for frequent traders, or for less liquid securities with wider
spreads.

## A practical takeaway

Wide bid-ask spreads are one signal (among several) that a security is
thinly traded -- worth noticing before placing a large market order in
something unfamiliar, since a market order in a wide-spread security can
execute at a noticeably worse price than the last quoted trade.

## Going deeper

A limit order (covered in a separate article) gives you direct control
over the exact price you're willing to accept, which is one common way
traders manage spread-related cost in less liquid securities.
