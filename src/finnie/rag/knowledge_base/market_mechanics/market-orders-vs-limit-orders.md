When you place a trade, you choose *how* you want it executed -- and the two
most common choices work quite differently.

## Market order

"Buy/sell this now, at whatever the current price is." A market order
almost always fills immediately, but you don't control the exact price --
in a fast-moving or thinly-traded stock, the price you actually get can
differ from the last quoted price by more than expected.

## Limit order

"Buy this, but only at this price or better" (or "sell, but only at this
price or better"). You control the price, but there's no guarantee the
order fills at all -- if the stock never reaches your limit price, the order
simply sits unfilled until it's cancelled or expires.

## A simple way to choose

Market orders suit situations where getting the trade done matters more
than the exact price -- for example, a highly liquid stock where prices
barely move between quote and execution. Limit orders suit situations where
price control matters more than certainty of execution -- for example, a
less liquid stock, or when you specifically want to buy only if the price
drops to a level you've decided is worthwhile.

## Other order types exist too

Stop orders, stop-limit orders, and others add more conditions (like
triggering only after a price crosses a certain level). These add
complexity and are generally worth understanding fully -- including their
specific risks -- before using them, rather than guessing at how they behave.

## Going deeper

For a long-term investor making periodic purchases of a diversified fund,
the difference between order types matters far less than it does for active
traders -- a simple market order is often perfectly adequate for that kind
of investing.
