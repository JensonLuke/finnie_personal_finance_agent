## A regulated venue for matching buyers and sellers

A stock exchange is an organized, regulated marketplace where buyers and
sellers of listed securities are matched. In the US, the two best-known are
the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) and Nasdaq -- but many others exist
around the world, each with its own listed companies and specific rules.

## Listing requirements

A company doesn't automatically qualify to have its shares trade on a major
exchange -- exchanges set requirements around financial size, reporting,
and governance that a company must meet and maintain to stay listed. A
company failing to meet these requirements can be "delisted," which
generally makes its shares much harder to trade.

## Exchanges vs. "the market" as a whole

"The stock market" isn't one single place -- it's really the combination of
all the exchanges (and other trading venues) where securities change
hands, plus the network of brokers and market makers connecting investors
to them. When your brokerage app shows you a quote, it's typically pulling
from this broader combined market, not necessarily a single exchange.

## Why regulation matters here

Exchanges operate under oversight from regulators (in the US, primarily the
SEC) that set rules around fair trading practices, disclosure, and market
manipulation. This regulatory structure is part of what gives public
markets a baseline level of transparency and investor protection that,
say, an informal private sale of shares wouldn't have.

## Going deeper

Not every company's shares trade on a major exchange -- some trade
"over-the-counter" (OTC) through a decentralized dealer network instead,
generally with lighter disclosure requirements and lower liquidity,
carrying meaningfully different risk characteristics than an exchange-
listed stock.
