Financial news constantly references a handful of recurring economic
figures. Here's what the most common ones actually measure, briefly.

## GDP (Gross Domestic Product)

The total value of goods and services produced in an economy over a given
period, usually reported as a growth (or contraction) rate compared to a
prior period. It's one of the broadest single measures of overall economic
activity, though it says little on its own about distribution -- how that
activity is spread across different people or regions.

## Inflation

The rate at which prices for goods and services rise over time, most
commonly tracked in the US via the Consumer Price Index (CPI). Even modest
inflation compounds over time, which is a core reason simply holding cash
long-term tends to lose purchasing power -- part of the argument for
investing rather than only saving in cash for long-term goals.

## Unemployment rate

The percentage of the labor force that's without a job and actively
looking for one. It's a lagging-ish indicator in the sense that it often
continues worsening for a while even after an economic recovery has begun,
since employers frequently wait to be confident growth will continue before
hiring back.

## Why these numbers move markets

These indicators feed directly into expectations about central bank policy
(particularly interest rates) and corporate earnings, which is why a single
economic data release can move markets meaningfully even though it doesn't
directly describe any specific company.

## Going deeper

No single indicator tells the whole story on its own -- economists and
investors typically look at several of these together, alongside their
trend over time, rather than reacting to any one report in isolation.
