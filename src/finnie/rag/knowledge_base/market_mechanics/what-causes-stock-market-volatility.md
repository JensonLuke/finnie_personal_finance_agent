## Markets are pricing in expectations, not just current facts

Stock prices reflect what investors collectively expect about a company's
or the economy's *future*, not just how things stand today. That means
prices can move sharply on news that changes expectations -- even without
anything having "actually happened" yet to a company's current operations.

## Common categories of volatility triggers

- **Economic data**: inflation reports, employment figures, GDP growth --
  these shift expectations about the broader economy and, often, about
  interest rates.
- **Central bank policy**: interest rate decisions ripple through
  borrowing costs and, in turn, through how investors value future company
  earnings.
- **Company-specific news**: earnings reports, product launches,
  leadership changes, legal issues.
- **Geopolitical events**: wars, elections, trade policy changes, and other
  events that shift the broader risk outlook.
- **Investor sentiment and psychology**: sometimes prices move on shifting
  mood and momentum alone, without a clear new piece of information behind
  the move.

## Why volatility can outpace the "actual news"

Markets often react not just to what happened, but to how it compares with
what was already expected -- a company can report genuinely strong profit
growth and still see its stock fall, if the market had expected even
stronger results. This "expectations versus reality" gap is a frequent
source of moves that seem disconnected from the underlying news at first
glance.

## Volatility is a normal, recurring feature, not a malfunction

Markets have always moved through periods of calm and periods of sharp
swings -- this isn't a sign something is broken, and it isn't unique to any
particular era.

## Going deeper

Because volatility is a normal and recurring feature of markets rather
than a rare malfunction, "how comfortable am I with volatility" is exactly
the kind of question a risk-tolerance assessment is meant to help answer
before it happens, not after.
