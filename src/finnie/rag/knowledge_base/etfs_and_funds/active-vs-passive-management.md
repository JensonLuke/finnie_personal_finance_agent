## The core distinction

A **passively managed** fund follows a fixed rule -- usually tracking an
index -- with no attempt to pick winners or time the market. An **actively
managed** fund has a manager or team making ongoing decisions about what to
buy, sell, and hold, aiming to outperform a benchmark index.

## Cost is the most consistent difference

Active management requires research staff and ongoing decision-making, so
active funds almost always charge higher expense ratios than passive index
funds tracking a similar market segment. That fee difference compounds over
decades, which is why cost is often the single most emphasized factor in
comparing the two.

## What the long-run evidence generally shows

Extensive research has repeatedly found that most actively managed funds
underperform a comparable low-cost index fund over long time periods, after
fees -- though a minority of active managers do outperform in any given
period, and past outperformance doesn't reliably predict future
outperformance. This evidence is the main reason passive/index investing
became the default recommendation many financial educators give beginners.

## Active management isn't automatically wrong

There are legitimate arguments for some active management -- for example,
in less efficiently priced markets, or for specific tax or risk-management
strategies. The point isn't that active management can never work; it's
that beating a low-cost index fund consistently, after fees, is genuinely
difficult even for professionals, which is a meaningfully different claim
than "impossible."

## Going deeper

Some funds blend the two approaches ("index-plus" or factor-based
strategies), applying systematic rules that aren't pure market-cap
weighting but also aren't fully discretionary human stock-picking --
another reason it's worth reading a fund's actual strategy rather than
assuming from its name alone.
