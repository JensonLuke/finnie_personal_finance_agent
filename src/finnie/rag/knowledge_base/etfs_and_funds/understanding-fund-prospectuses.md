## What a prospectus actually is

Every mutual fund and ETF registered with the SEC is required to publish a
prospectus -- a legal document that discloses the fund's investment
objective, strategy, risks, fees, and past performance. It's dense and not
written for easy reading, but it's the single most complete source of
information about what a fund actually does.

## The parts worth actually reading

- **Investment objective and strategy**: what the fund is trying to
  achieve and how -- confirms the fund does what its name and marketing
  suggest.
- **Fee table**: the expense ratio, plus any other fees (some funds carry
  sales charges called "loads," though these are less common in ETFs).
- **Principal risks**: a plain-language (if dense) list of what could go
  wrong -- market risk, concentration risk, specific risks tied to the
  fund's strategy.
- **Past performance**: required to include a disclaimer that past
  performance doesn't guarantee future results -- worth taking seriously,
  not just skimming past.

## A shorter alternative: the summary prospectus

Most funds also publish a "summary prospectus" -- a condensed version
covering the essentials in a few pages, meant to be far more approachable
than the full legal document while still being an official disclosure.

## Why this matters even for a simple index fund

Even a plain index fund's prospectus is worth a quick check -- confirming
it actually tracks the index you think it does, its exact expense ratio,
and whether it uses any techniques (like securities lending) that add a
small amount of additional risk beyond simply holding the index's
components.

## Going deeper

A fund's prospectus is updated periodically (often annually), so it's worth
checking you're looking at a current version, especially if a fund's
strategy or fee structure has changed since you first researched it.
