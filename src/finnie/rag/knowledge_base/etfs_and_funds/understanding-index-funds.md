## The basic idea

An index fund doesn't try to pick winning stocks. Instead, it simply buys
all (or a representative sample) of the stocks in a market index -- like the
S&P 500, which tracks 500 large US companies -- in roughly the same
proportions as the index itself. Its goal is to *match* the market's return,
not beat it.

## Why "just matching the market" is popular advice

Decades of research have found that most professional fund managers who try
to beat the market underperform a simple index fund over long time periods,
after fees are accounted for. Index funds also charge much lower fees than
actively managed funds, since there's no team of analysts trying to pick
stocks -- the fund just follows a formula.

## What "the market" means here

There's no single "the market" -- there are many indexes, each tracking a
different slice: large US companies, small US companies, international
stocks, bonds, and so on. A "total market" index fund aims to hold nearly
every publicly traded company in its target market, which is about as
diversified as a single fund can get.

## What index funds don't protect you from

An index fund still falls when the overall market falls -- it offers
diversification *within* the market it tracks, not protection *from* market
declines generally. If the S&P 500 drops 20%, an S&P 500 index fund drops
roughly 20% too.

## Going deeper

Index investing is a philosophy as much as a product choice: instead of
trying to guess which companies or fund managers will outperform, you accept
the market's average return, keep costs low, and let compounding do the
work over time.
