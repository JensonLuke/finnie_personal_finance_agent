Both ETFs (exchange-traded funds) and mutual funds pool money from many
investors into a basket of holdings -- but they work differently under the
hood.

## How they trade

An ETF trades on an exchange all day long, just like a stock -- its price
moves continuously as people buy and sell it. A mutual fund is priced only
once, after markets close each day; every buy or sell order placed during
the day executes at that one end-of-day price (called the NAV, or net asset
value).

## Minimums and access

Many mutual funds have a minimum initial investment (sometimes $500-$3,000
or more). ETFs typically don't -- you can often buy a single share, or even
a fraction of one through many modern brokerages, making them more
accessible for someone just starting out with a small amount.

## Costs

Both can be actively managed or passively track an index, but ETFs have
historically tended to run lower expense ratios on average, partly because
so many of the largest ETFs are simple index trackers. Always check the
actual expense ratio of the specific fund -- the ETF-vs-mutual-fund label
alone doesn't guarantee which is cheaper.

## Taxes

In a regular (non-retirement) taxable account, ETFs are often more
tax-efficient than mutual funds because of how they're structured -- they
tend to generate fewer taxable capital gains distributions in a typical
year. This difference matters much less inside a tax-advantaged account
like a 401(k) or IRA.

## Going deeper

For a beginner building a simple, diversified starting portfolio, the choice
between a broad-market index ETF and an equivalent broad-market index mutual
fund often comes down to personal preference and what's available in your
specific account -- the underlying investment can be nearly identical.
