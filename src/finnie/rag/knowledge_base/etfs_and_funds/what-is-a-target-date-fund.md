## A single fund that adjusts itself over time

A target-date fund is built around an expected retirement year (its name
usually includes it, like "Target Retirement 2055 Fund"). It holds a mix of
stocks and bonds that starts more aggressive (more stocks) when the target
date is far away, and automatically shifts to be more conservative (more
bonds) as that date approaches -- a process called the fund's "glide path."

## Why they're popular as a default option

Target-date funds are designed to be a complete, standalone retirement
investment -- you're not meant to pair one with a separate stock-picking
strategy, though some people do combine one with other holdings. Because
they handle the stock/bond mix and the gradual shift automatically, they've
become a common default option in employer 401(k) plans for people who
don't want to actively manage their own allocation.

## What to check before choosing one

- **The glide path**: how aggressively it shifts and when -- different
  fund families make different assumptions, so a "2055 fund" from one
  provider isn't necessarily identical to another provider's.
- **The underlying funds and expense ratio**: a target-date fund is often
  built from several underlying index funds, and the combined expense ratio
  varies by provider.
- **"To" vs. "through" retirement**: some target-date funds reach their
  most conservative mix *at* the target date; others continue shifting for
  years *after* it, assuming you'll keep the money invested well into
  retirement rather than withdrawing it all at once.

## Going deeper

A target-date fund's "target year" doesn't have to exactly match your
actual planned retirement year -- some investors deliberately choose a
later-dated (more aggressive) or earlier-dated (more conservative) fund
than their real timeline suggests, based on their personal risk tolerance.
