## A Roth option inside a workplace plan

Many employer 401(k) plans now offer a Roth option alongside the
traditional one. The mechanics of participating are the same -- automatic
payroll contributions into an employer-sponsored account -- but the tax
treatment follows the Roth model: contributions are made with money you've
already paid tax on, and qualified withdrawals in retirement, including all
the growth, are tax-free.

## Roth 401(k) vs. Roth IRA

They share the same "pay tax now, not later" logic, but with two notable
differences: a Roth 401(k) has no income limit restricting who can
contribute (unlike a Roth IRA), and its contribution limit is the same
(much higher) limit that applies to 401(k)s generally, rather than the
lower IRA limit. This makes a Roth 401(k) a way for higher earners to get
Roth-style tax treatment at a larger scale than a Roth IRA alone would
allow.

## The employer match is still pre-tax

Even if you contribute to the Roth side of your 401(k), any employer
matching contribution is generally deposited into a traditional
(pre-tax) portion of your account, not the Roth portion -- something worth
knowing so the numbers on your account statement make sense.

## Choosing between traditional and Roth contributions

The same "which tax bracket now vs. in retirement" reasoning used for
IRAs applies here too. Some plans let you split contributions between
traditional and Roth, which can be a reasonable way to hedge against
uncertainty about future tax rates rather than betting entirely on one
outcome.

## Going deeper

Not every employer offers a Roth 401(k) option -- it depends on how the
plan is set up. If yours doesn't, a separate Roth IRA (subject to its own
income limits and lower contribution cap) may be the more direct way to get
similar tax treatment.
