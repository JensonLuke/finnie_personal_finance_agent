## What it is

A 401(k) is a retirement savings plan offered through your employer. You
elect to have a percentage of each paycheck contributed automatically,
before it's taxed, into an investment account you control (within the
options your employer's plan offers).

## The employer match

Many employers match some portion of what you contribute -- a common
formula is "50% of the first 6% you contribute." If your employer offers a
match and you're not contributing enough to get all of it, you're leaving
free money on the table; it's usually one of the highest-value moves in
personal finance, ahead of most other saving or investing decisions.

## Tax treatment

Traditional 401(k) contributions reduce your taxable income now, and the
money grows tax-deferred -- you pay income tax when you eventually withdraw
it in retirement. Many plans also offer a Roth 401(k) option, where you
contribute after-tax money but qualified withdrawals in retirement are
tax-free. Some plans let you split contributions between both.

## Contribution limits and early withdrawals

The IRS sets an annual limit on how much you can contribute, adjusted most
years for inflation -- check irs.gov for the current year's number rather
than relying on a fixed figure, since it changes. Withdrawing before age
59½ generally triggers both ordinary income tax and a 10% early-withdrawal
penalty, with some narrow exceptions.

## Going deeper

A 401(k)'s investment menu is usually limited to a curated list of funds
chosen by the plan administrator -- often index funds and target-date
funds -- rather than the full range of stocks and ETFs you'd have in a
personal brokerage account or IRA.
