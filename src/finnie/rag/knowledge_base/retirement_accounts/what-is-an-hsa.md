## Not a retirement account by name, but often used like one

A Health Savings Account is technically for healthcare expenses, not
retirement -- but its tax treatment is distinctive enough, and its rules
flexible enough, that many people use it as a supplementary long-term
savings vehicle alongside their retirement accounts.

## Eligibility: tied to your health insurance

You can only contribute to an HSA if you're enrolled in a qualifying
high-deductible health plan (HDHP). If you don't have an HDHP, you're not
eligible to contribute, regardless of how appealing the account's tax
treatment sounds.

## The "triple tax advantage"

- Contributions are tax-deductible (or pre-tax, if made through payroll).
- Growth inside the account is tax-free.
- Withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are also tax-free.

No other common account type combines all three of these tax breaks at
once, which is why HSAs are often highlighted as unusually tax-efficient.

## Why some people invest their HSA rather than spend it

Many HSA providers let you invest the balance (similar to an IRA) once it
exceeds a minimum threshold, rather than just holding it in cash for
near-term medical bills. Unlike a Flexible Spending Account (FSA), HSA
funds roll over year to year with no "use it or lose it" deadline, and the
account stays with you even if you change jobs or health plans.

## Using it in retirement

After age 65, HSA funds can be withdrawn for any purpose, not just medical
expenses -- non-medical withdrawals after that age are simply taxed as
ordinary income, similar to a Traditional IRA, without an early-withdrawal
penalty. Withdrawals for qualified medical expenses remain tax-free at any
age.

## Going deeper

Because HSA eligibility depends entirely on your health plan, it's worth
confirming your specific plan actually qualifies as an HDHP under current
IRS rules before contributing, rather than assuming any high-deductible-
sounding plan automatically qualifies.
