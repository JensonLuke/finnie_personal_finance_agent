## The government eventually wants its share

Traditional 401(k)s and Traditional IRAs let your contributions grow
tax-deferred for decades -- but that deferral isn't indefinite. Starting at
an age set by law (which has been adjusted upward in recent years, so check
current IRS guidance rather than assuming a fixed number), you're required
to withdraw a minimum amount each year, whether you need the money or not.
These withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income.

## How the amount is calculated

The IRS publishes life-expectancy tables, and your RMD each year is roughly
your account balance divided by a factor from that table based on your age
-- meaning the required percentage withdrawn generally increases as you get
older.

## The penalty for missing one

Failing to withdraw the full required amount by the deadline can trigger a
significant excise tax penalty on the shortfall. If this ever applies to
you, it's worth confirming the current-year rules directly rather than
assuming last year's numbers still apply, since RMD rules have changed more
than once in recent years.

## Roth accounts and RMDs

Roth IRAs held by the original owner are not subject to RMDs during their
lifetime -- one of their distinguishing advantages. Roth 401(k)s previously
were subject to RMDs but that rule has since changed; this is an area where
checking current guidance matters, since it's shifted.

## Going deeper

RMDs are one of the more specific, rule-heavy corners of retirement
accounts, and the exact figures and starting age are exactly the kind of
detail that's worth confirming with a tax professional or the IRS's own
current-year guidance rather than relying on a general education summary
like this one.
