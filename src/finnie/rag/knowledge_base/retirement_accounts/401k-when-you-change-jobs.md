## The money is yours -- the account logistics are what change

Leaving a job doesn't mean losing your 401(k) balance (your own
contributions are always fully yours; employer matching contributions may
be subject to a "vesting schedule" that determines how much of that portion
you keep if you leave early). What changes is that you generally have a few
options for what to do with the account itself.

## The main options

1. **Leave it where it is**, if your former employer's plan allows former
   employees to keep balances there (many do, above a minimum balance).
2. **Roll it over into your new employer's 401(k)**, if the new plan
   accepts incoming rollovers.
3. **Roll it over into an IRA**, which often unlocks a much wider range of
   investment choices than a workplace plan offers.
4. **Cash it out** -- generally the option to avoid: it typically triggers
   income tax on the full amount plus, if you're under 59½, an additional
   10% early-withdrawal penalty.

## Why a "direct rollover" matters

When rolling funds over, requesting a **direct rollover** -- where the money
moves straight from the old plan to the new account without passing through
your hands -- avoids a mandatory 20% withholding that applies to indirect
rollovers, and avoids the strict 60-day window you'd otherwise have to
redeposit the funds to avoid taxes and penalties.

## Old 401(k)s add up

It's common to accumulate several old 401(k)s scattered across former
employers over a career. Consolidating them (via rollover into an IRA or a
current employer's plan) isn't required, but it can make tracking
allocation, fees, and overall diversification considerably simpler than
managing several separate accounts.

## Going deeper

Before rolling over, it's worth comparing the investment options and fees
of the destination account against what you currently have -- a rollover
isn't automatically an improvement if it moves you into a plan with higher
fees or a more limited investment menu.
