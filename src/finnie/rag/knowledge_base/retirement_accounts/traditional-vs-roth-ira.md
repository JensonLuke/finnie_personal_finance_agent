Both are personal retirement accounts you open yourself, independent of an
employer. The core difference is *when* you pay tax.

## Traditional IRA

Contributions may be tax-deductible now (depending on your income and
whether you or a spouse also have a workplace plan), and the money grows
tax-deferred. You pay ordinary income tax on withdrawals in retirement.

## Roth IRA

Contributions are made with money you've already paid tax on -- no
upfront deduction. In exchange, qualified withdrawals in retirement,
including all the growth, come out completely tax-free. Roth IRAs also have
income limits on who can contribute directly, which Traditional IRAs don't.

## A simple way to think about the choice

If you expect to be in a *higher* tax bracket in retirement than you are
now, paying tax later (Traditional) can cost more than paying it now (Roth).
If you expect a *lower* bracket in retirement, the reverse can be true. Many
beginners without a strong prediction either way choose Roth for the
simplicity of tax-free withdrawals and no required distributions during
their lifetime.

## Contribution limits are shared

The IRS sets one combined annual contribution limit across your Traditional
and Roth IRAs together, not a separate limit for each -- adjusted most years
for inflation. Check irs.gov for the current figure.

## Going deeper

You're not locked into one type forever, and some people use both --
splitting contributions, or doing a "Roth conversion" of Traditional IRA
funds later. Those moves have their own tax consequences and are worth
discussing with a tax professional for your specific situation.
