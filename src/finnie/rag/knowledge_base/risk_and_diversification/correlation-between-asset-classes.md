## The idea diversification actually depends on

Diversification only reduces risk if the things you're combining don't all
move in exactly the same direction at the same time. Correlation is the
statistical measure of how closely two investments' movements track each
other, on a scale from -1 (move in perfectly opposite directions) through 0
(no consistent relationship) to +1 (move in perfect lockstep).

## Why "own more stocks" isn't automatically "diversified"

Owning 50 different technology stocks is much less diversifying than
owning 50 stocks spread across many different sectors, because stocks
within the same sector tend to be more highly correlated with each other --
they often respond to similar news and economic conditions in similar
ways. True diversification benefit comes from combining assets with lower
correlation to each other, not simply from owning a larger number of
things.

## Correlation isn't fixed

An important, sometimes uncomfortable fact: correlations between asset
classes can shift, and often rise, during severe market stress -- exactly
when investors most want their diversification to work. Assets that
normally move somewhat independently can temporarily move together during
a broad panic, a phenomenon sometimes summarized as "in a real crisis,
everything correlates."

## Bonds and stocks: a commonly cited (but not guaranteed) relationship

Bonds and stocks have historically often shown low or even negative
correlation, which is part of the traditional rationale for holding both --
but this relationship isn't fixed or guaranteed to hold in every period or
every kind of economic environment, including periods when both have
declined together.

## Going deeper

Correlation is a backward-looking statistical measure based on historical
data, not a law of nature -- it's a useful tool for building a diversified
portfolio, not a guarantee about how any two asset classes will behave
together in the future.
