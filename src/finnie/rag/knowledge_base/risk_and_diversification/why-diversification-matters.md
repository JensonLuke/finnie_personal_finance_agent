## The core idea

Diversification means spreading your money across many different
investments so that any single one's bad outcome doesn't wipe out your
whole portfolio. It works because different investments don't all move
together -- when one sector or asset type struggles, another may hold
steady or even gain.

## Diversifying across more than one dimension

Real diversification isn't just "own more than one stock" -- it means
spreading across:

- **Companies**: many individual businesses, not just a few.
- **Sectors**: technology, healthcare, energy, financials, and so on --
  concentrating in one sector ties your outcome to that sector's fate.
- **Asset classes**: stocks, bonds, and cash behave differently, especially
  during downturns.
- **Geography**: US and international markets don't always move in sync.

## What diversification does and doesn't do

Diversification reduces the risk of any single company or sector
devastating your portfolio. It does **not** eliminate risk entirely --
a well-diversified portfolio still falls when the overall market falls, and
there's no combination of investments that guarantees a profit or protects
fully against loss.

## The easy way to diversify

Buying a single individual stock is the opposite of diversified. A
broad-market index fund or ETF, on the other hand, can give you exposure to
hundreds or thousands of companies in one purchase -- which is a big reason
they're commonly recommended as a simple starting point for beginners.

## Going deeper

"Diversification score" style metrics (like the one this assistant computes
for your portfolio) are a simplified heuristic, not a complete risk
assessment -- they're a useful starting signal, not the final word on
whether your specific mix of holdings is right for your goals.
