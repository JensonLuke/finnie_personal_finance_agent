## Two different kinds of risk tolerance

**Risk capacity** is how much risk you can financially afford to take, given
your timeline and situation -- someone investing for retirement 30 years out
generally has more capacity for short-term ups and downs than someone
saving for a house down payment next year.

**Risk comfort** is how much volatility you can handle emotionally without
making a panicked decision, like selling everything during a market drop.
These two don't always match -- you might have the financial capacity for an
aggressive portfolio but lose sleep over its swings, or the reverse.

## Why this matters more than picking "the best" investment

A portfolio that's theoretically optimal but that you abandon during a
downturn will underperform a "good enough" portfolio you actually stick
with. Matching your investments to a risk level you can genuinely tolerate,
not just what looks best on paper, is often the more important decision.

## Rough categories

- **Conservative**: prioritizes protecting what you have, tends toward more
  bonds and cash, accepts lower expected long-term growth in exchange for
  smaller swings.
- **Moderate**: a mix of stocks and bonds aiming to balance growth and
  stability.
- **Aggressive**: leans heavily toward stocks for higher expected long-term
  growth, while accepting larger short-term swings.

These are starting points, not rigid rules -- and they typically shift as
your timeline, goals, and life circumstances change.

## Going deeper

Time horizon and risk tolerance are closely linked: the longer you have
before you need the money, the more time a portfolio has to recover from a
downturn, which is why longer-term goals can often support a somewhat higher
risk tolerance than short-term ones.
