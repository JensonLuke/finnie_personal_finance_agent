## What the word actually measures

In investing, volatility refers to how much and how quickly a price moves
up and down over a given period -- not whether the price is trending up or
down, just how bumpy the ride is. A stock that swings wildly in both
directions is "volatile" even if it ends up flat over the year; a stock
that rises slowly and steadily has low volatility.

## Volatility isn't the same thing as "bad"

It's easy to equate volatility with risk of loss, but they're related, not
identical. A highly volatile investment could just as easily swing in your
favor as against you -- the defining feature is the size and frequency of
the swings, not their direction. That said, higher volatility does
generally mean a wider range of possible short-term outcomes, which is a
real form of risk for anyone who might need the money on short notice.

## Why diversification reduces volatility

Combining assets that don't move perfectly in sync (different sectors,
different asset classes) tends to smooth out a portfolio's overall
volatility compared to holding any single volatile piece alone -- gains in
one holding can offset losses in another, even if you can't predict which
will do which in advance.

## Volatility and time horizon

A short-term volatile swing matters far more if you need the money soon
than if you have decades before you'll touch it -- the same volatile asset
carries very different practical risk depending on when you'll actually
need to convert it back to cash.

## Going deeper

Volatility can be measured numerically (standard deviation of returns is
the common statistical measure), which is how many fund fact sheets and
robo-advisor risk assessments quantify "how bumpy has this holding's ride
been historically" -- useful context, though historical volatility doesn't
guarantee future volatility will look the same.
