## The mix, not just the pieces

Asset allocation refers to how your money is divided across broad asset
categories -- typically stocks, bonds, and cash -- rather than which
specific stocks or funds you hold within each category. It's often
described as the single biggest driver of a portfolio's overall risk and
return characteristics, more so than the specific securities chosen within
each category.

## The general risk/return trade-off by category

- **Stocks**: historically the highest long-run average returns, paired
  with the largest short-term swings.
- **Bonds**: historically more modest returns, with generally smaller
  swings than stocks.
- **Cash**: minimal risk of loss in nominal terms, but the lowest expected
  return, and often doesn't keep pace with inflation over time.

There's no allocation that's "correct" for everyone -- the right mix
depends on timeline, goals, and risk tolerance, discussed in more detail in
this assistant's other articles on that topic.

## Common rules of thumb (starting points, not rigid formulas)

One traditional rule of thumb suggested holding a percentage in bonds equal
to your age (a 30-year-old holding 30% bonds, 70% stocks) -- a simple
heuristic, not a scientifically optimized formula, and one that's been
debated and revised over time as life expectancies and retirement patterns
have changed.

## Allocation vs. diversification

These are related but distinct: allocation is the mix *across* categories
(stocks vs. bonds vs. cash); diversification is the spread *within* a
category (many different stocks and sectors, rather than a few). Both
matter -- a portfolio can be well-allocated across asset classes but still
poorly diversified within one of them.

## Going deeper

Because different asset classes grow at different rates, a portfolio's
actual allocation drifts away from its original target over time without
intervention -- which is exactly the problem periodic rebalancing (covered
in a separate article) is meant to address.
