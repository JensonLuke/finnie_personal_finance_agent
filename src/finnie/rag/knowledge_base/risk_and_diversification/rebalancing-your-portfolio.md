## Why a portfolio drifts on its own

If you set a target of 70% stocks / 30% bonds and never touch it again, a
strong stock market run can shift that mix to, say, 80/20 purely because
stocks grew faster -- without you making any active decision to take on
more risk. Rebalancing means periodically buying and selling to bring the
portfolio back toward its original target mix.

## What rebalancing actually involves

In its simplest form: sell a portion of whatever has grown to be
overweight, and use it to buy more of whatever has become underweight,
until the mix matches your target again. Done consistently, this has a
secondary effect some investors like: it systematically involves selling
somewhat more of what's recently risen and buying somewhat more of what's
recently lagged -- a disciplined, unemotional version of "sell high, buy
low," though it's not a market-timing strategy and shouldn't be treated as
one.

## Common ways to rebalance

- **On a schedule**: once a year, or every six months, regardless of how
  far the allocation has drifted.
- **By threshold**: whenever an asset class drifts more than a set
  percentage (e.g. 5 percentage points) away from its target.
- **Using new contributions**: directing new money toward whichever asset
  class is currently underweight, rather than selling existing holdings --
  often the simplest and most tax-efficient method in a taxable account.

## Tax considerations

Rebalancing by selling appreciated holdings in a taxable account can
trigger capital gains tax. This is one more reason rebalancing via new
contributions, or doing the selling inside a tax-advantaged account where
gains aren't immediately taxed, is often preferred when the option exists.

## Going deeper

Target-date funds (covered in a separate article) rebalance automatically
as part of their design -- one of the conveniences that makes them
appealing to investors who'd rather not manage this process by hand.
