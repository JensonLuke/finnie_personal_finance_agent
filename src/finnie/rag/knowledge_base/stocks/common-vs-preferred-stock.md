## Two different classes of ownership

Most of the time when people talk about "buying stock," they mean common
stock -- the type most beginners encounter. But some companies also issue
preferred stock, a different class with its own trade-offs.

## Common stock

Common stockholders typically get voting rights (one vote per share at
shareholder meetings) and a claim on any dividends the company chooses to
pay -- but dividends aren't guaranteed, and common stockholders are paid
last if a company goes bankrupt and its assets are liquidated.

## Preferred stock

Preferred stockholders usually give up voting rights in exchange for two
things: a fixed dividend rate that's generally paid before common
stockholders get anything, and a higher claim on assets than common stock
(though still behind bondholders) if the company is liquidated. Preferred
stock behaves a bit like a hybrid between a stock and a bond -- its price
tends to be less volatile than common stock, and it trades more on interest
rate expectations than on the company's growth prospects.

## Why beginners usually own common stock

Preferred stock is less commonly available to individual investors and is
more often held by institutions. Broad-market index funds -- the typical
starting point for beginners -- are made up almost entirely of common
stock, so most new investors are common stockholders without ever having
had to choose.

## Going deeper

If you ever see "preferred" in a fund or company's name, don't assume it's
automatically the "better" option -- the name refers to this specific class
structure, not to investment quality. Which class suits an investor depends
on what they're prioritizing: growth and voting rights (common) or steadier
income and a higher liquidation claim (preferred).
