## Two common ways to categorize stocks

Beyond sector (tech, healthcare, and so on), investors often group stocks
into "growth" and "value" styles -- a distinction that shows up constantly in
fund names ("Large-Cap Growth ETF," "Small-Cap Value Fund").

## Growth stocks

Companies expected to grow revenue and earnings faster than average, often
reinvesting profits back into the business rather than paying dividends.
Growth stocks tend to trade at a higher price relative to their current
earnings (a higher "P/E ratio"), because investors are paying up front for
expected future growth. They can be more volatile -- if growth expectations
disappoint, the price can fall sharply.

## Value stocks

Companies that appear cheap relative to fundamentals like earnings or
assets, often more mature businesses in established industries. Value
stocks more commonly pay dividends and tend to trade at lower P/E ratios.
The investing thesis is that the market has temporarily undervalued the
company relative to its actual worth.

## Neither style is "safer" by default

It's a common misconception that value stocks are automatically lower-risk
than growth stocks. Both styles carry real risk -- growth stocks risk
disappointing on expectations that were already priced in; value stocks risk
being cheap for a legitimate reason (a struggling business) rather than
being a bargain.

## Blended approaches

Many broad-market index funds hold a mix of both styles in proportion to
how the overall market is composed, rather than tilting toward one. This is
part of why a simple total-market index fund is often recommended for
beginners -- it doesn't require picking a style, or a company, correctly.

## Going deeper

These labels aren't a strict binary -- many companies fall somewhere in
between, and how a stock gets classified as "growth" or "value" can vary
between different index providers and fund managers using different
criteria.
