## An informal, not official, category

"Blue-chip" is a widely used term with no strict legal definition -- it
generally refers to large, well-established, financially stable companies
with a long track record, often (though not always) household names. The
term reportedly comes from poker, where blue chips traditionally carry the
highest value.

## Common traits of blue-chip companies

- Large market capitalization -- among the biggest companies in their
  market.
- A long operating history, having weathered multiple economic cycles.
- Often (not always) a history of paying steady or growing dividends.
- Generally considered lower-risk than smaller, newer companies, though
  "lower-risk" doesn't mean "risk-free" -- blue-chip companies can and do
  decline, sometimes significantly.

## Why beginners hear about them often

Blue-chip stocks are frequently used as introductory examples precisely
because their business models tend to be easier to understand than a
speculative smaller company's, and their long histories give more data to
look at. That doesn't automatically make them a better investment choice
for any particular person -- it depends on someone's goals, timeline, and
overall portfolio.

## Index inclusion

Blue-chip companies make up a large share of major indexes like the S&P 500
and the Dow Jones Industrial Average, which is itself made up of just 30
large, well-known companies. Owning a broad index fund gives exposure to
most blue-chip companies (and many smaller ones) without needing to pick
individual names.

## Going deeper

"Blue-chip" describes reputation and size, not a guarantee of future
performance -- past stability is not a promise about what happens next, and
concentrating heavily in even well-established companies still means giving
up the risk-reduction benefit of broader diversification.
