## The basic mechanic

A stock split increases the number of shares outstanding while
proportionally reducing the price per share -- the total value of what you
own doesn't change. In a common "2-for-1" split, each existing share becomes
two shares, and the price per share roughly halves. If you owned 10 shares
at $200 each ($2,000 total) before a 2-for-1 split, you'd own 20 shares at
roughly $100 each ($2,000 total) right after.

## Why companies split their stock

Often, simply to make each share more affordable-looking and improve
trading accessibility, especially after a stock's price has climbed to a
level that feels psychologically expensive to smaller investors. Some
companies also believe a lower per-share price encourages more active
trading, though the evidence for that effect is mixed.

## A stock split doesn't change fundamentals

This is the single most important thing to understand: a split doesn't add
any value to the company, doesn't change its earnings, and doesn't change
your proportional ownership stake. It's often compared to cutting a pizza
into more slices -- you have more pieces, but not more pizza.

## Reverse splits

The opposite also happens: a reverse split (e.g. "1-for-10") reduces the
number of shares and proportionally increases the price per share. Companies
sometimes do this to avoid being delisted from an exchange that requires a
minimum share price, or to make the stock look less "penny stock"-like.
Reverse splits carry a slightly more cautious reputation among investors,
since they're sometimes associated with a struggling stock.

## Going deeper

Because index funds and ETFs are priced in shares of the fund itself (not
directly tied to the split history of underlying holdings), a stock split
inside one of your fund's holdings doesn't require you to do anything -- the
fund handles the adjustment automatically.
