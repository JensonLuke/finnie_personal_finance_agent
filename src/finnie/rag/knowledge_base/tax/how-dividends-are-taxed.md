## Two categories, taxed differently

Dividends fall into two tax categories, and the difference matters:

- **Qualified dividends** are taxed at the same lower rates as long-term
  capital gains. To qualify, the dividend generally has to come from a US
  company or a qualifying foreign one, and you generally need to have held
  the underlying stock for a minimum holding period around the dividend
  date.
- **Ordinary (non-qualified) dividends** are taxed as ordinary income, at
  your regular tax bracket -- the same as wages.

Your brokerage's year-end tax form (Form 1099-DIV) breaks out how much of
what you received falls into each category, so you don't have to determine
this yourself.

## Reinvested dividends are still taxable

A common point of confusion: if your dividends are automatically reinvested
into more shares rather than paid out to you in cash, they're still taxable
income in the year they're paid, in a regular taxable account. The
reinvestment doesn't defer the tax -- it does, however, increase your cost
basis in the investment, which matters later when calculating capital
gains on eventual sale.

## Dividends inside tax-advantaged accounts

Inside a 401(k), Traditional IRA, or Roth IRA, dividends aren't taxed as
they're received at all -- they follow the account's overall tax treatment
instead (deferred for Traditional accounts, tax-free for Roth). This is one
more reason account type matters as much as investment choice.

## Going deeper

Dividend-paying stocks and funds aren't automatically more or less
tax-efficient than growth-focused ones in every situation -- it depends on
account type, your tax bracket, and whether the dividends are qualified or
not. It's worth understanding rather than assuming either "dividends are
great" or "dividends are a tax problem" as a blanket rule.
