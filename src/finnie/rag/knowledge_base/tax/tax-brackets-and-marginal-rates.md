## The most common misunderstanding about tax brackets

Many beginners assume that moving into a higher tax bracket means *all* of
their income gets taxed at that higher rate. That's not how it works: the
US uses a marginal, progressive system, where each bracket's rate only
applies to the portion of income that falls within that specific bracket.

## A simplified illustration

Say (using simplified, illustrative numbers) the first $10,000 of income is
taxed at 10%, and income from $10,000-$40,000 is taxed at 12%. Someone
earning $40,000 doesn't pay 12% on the full $40,000 -- they pay 10% on the
first $10,000 and 12% only on the remaining $30,000. Their *effective* tax
rate (total tax divided by total income) ends up lower than their top
("marginal") bracket rate.

## Why "getting a raise pushes me into a higher bracket" isn't scary

Because only the income *within* the new, higher bracket is taxed at that
higher rate, a raise that pushes part of your income into a higher bracket
essentially never results in taking home less money overall -- a common
but mistaken worry.

## Marginal rate vs. effective rate

Your **marginal rate** is the rate on your last dollar earned (your top
bracket). Your **effective rate** is your total tax divided by total
income, which is always lower than your marginal rate under this system.
Effective rate is the more meaningful number for understanding your actual
overall tax burden.

## Going deeper

Tax bracket thresholds are adjusted periodically (often annually, for
inflation), and filing status (single, married filing jointly, etc.)
changes where the thresholds fall -- always check current IRS figures for
your specific filing status rather than relying on a fixed set of numbers.
