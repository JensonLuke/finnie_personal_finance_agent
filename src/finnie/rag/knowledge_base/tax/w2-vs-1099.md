## Two different working relationships, two different forms

These forms reflect *how* you were classified for a given source of
income, not just a paperwork formality -- the classification changes how
taxes are withheld and what you owe.

## W-2: employee

If you're a traditional employee, your employer sends you a W-2 each year
summarizing your wages and how much was already withheld for federal
income tax, Social Security, and Medicare throughout the year. Your
employer pays half of your Social Security and Medicare tax on your behalf
("payroll tax"); you're responsible for the other half, generally withheld
automatically from each paycheck.

## 1099: independent contractor / self-employed

If you did work as a freelancer, contractor, or through your own business
for a client, you may receive a 1099 form (several variants exist,
depending on the type of income) reporting what you were paid -- with no
tax withheld at all. As a self-employed person, you're responsible for both
halves of Social Security and Medicare tax yourself (called
"self-employment tax"), on top of regular income tax.

## Why this catches new freelancers off guard

Because nothing is withheld from 1099 income throughout the year, it's easy
to underestimate what you'll owe come tax time if you haven't been setting
money aside. This is part of why self-employed individuals are generally
expected to make estimated quarterly tax payments throughout the year
rather than paying it all at once in April.

## Retirement account access differs too

W-2 employees may have access to an employer 401(k); 1099 self-employed
income opens up self-employed-specific options like a SEP IRA or Solo
401(k) instead, discussed in a separate article.

## Going deeper

Worker classification (employee vs. contractor) isn't simply chosen by
preference -- there are legal criteria for which one actually applies to a
given work arrangement, and misclassification carries real consequences
for both workers and businesses.
