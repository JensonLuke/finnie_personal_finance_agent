## The basic rule

You only owe capital gains tax when you *sell* an investment for more than
you paid for it -- an unrealized gain (a stock that's gone up but that you
still hold) isn't taxed. The gain is the difference between your sale price
and your "cost basis" (generally what you originally paid, including any
reinvested dividends).

## Short-term vs. long-term

Sell something you've held for **one year or less**, and the gain is taxed
as ordinary income, at your regular tax bracket. Hold it for **more than a
year**, and it qualifies for long-term capital gains rates, which are lower
than ordinary income tax rates for most people. This one-year line is a
meaningful incentive to hold investments longer rather than trade
frequently.

## Losses can offset gains

If you sell some investments at a loss, those losses can offset gains
elsewhere, and often a limited amount of ordinary income too, reducing your
overall tax bill -- a strategy sometimes called "tax-loss harvesting."
Rules around this (like the "wash sale" rule, which disallows the loss if
you buy back a substantially identical investment too soon) are specific
enough that this is a good area to involve a tax professional.

## Retirement accounts change the picture entirely

Inside a Traditional or Roth IRA or 401(k), buying and selling investments
doesn't trigger capital gains tax at all -- tax is handled differently (or
not at all, for Roth) when you eventually withdraw. Capital gains tax is
purely a taxable-brokerage-account concept.

## Going deeper

This is general education, not tax advice for your specific return --
capital gains rules involve income thresholds and details that change from
year to year, and a tax professional or the IRS's own guidance is the right
place for anything specific to your situation.
