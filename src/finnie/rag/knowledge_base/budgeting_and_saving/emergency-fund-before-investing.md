## Why order matters

It's tempting to jump straight into investing, but most financial educators
recommend building a basic emergency fund first. Here's the reasoning: if an
unexpected expense hits -- a car repair, a medical bill, a job loss -- and
your only money is tied up in investments, you might be forced to sell at a
bad time, potentially locking in a loss, just to cover it.

## How much is "enough"

A commonly cited starting target is **3-6 months of essential expenses** --
rent/mortgage, utilities, food, insurance, minimum debt payments -- kept
somewhere safe and easy to access, like a high-yield savings account. People
with less stable income or job security sometimes aim for the higher end of
that range, or beyond.

## Why not just invest the emergency fund too

Investments can lose value in the short term, and stocks in particular can
be down significantly at exactly the moment you might need the money most
(a recession often brings both market declines and job losses at the same
time). An emergency fund's job is stability and immediate access, not
growth -- that's a different job than what a long-term investment portfolio
is for.

## A simple sequencing framework

1. Cover any high-interest debt and get any employer 401(k) match.
2. Build a starter emergency fund (even one month's expenses is progress).
3. Build up to the full 3-6 month target.
4. Direct additional savings toward longer-term investing goals.

Real life rarely fits a strict sequence like this perfectly, and that's
fine -- the point is having *some* buffer before taking on investment risk
with money you might need on short notice.

## Going deeper

An emergency fund isn't "wasted" money just because it's not invested --
its value is the flexibility and peace of mind it buys you, which is what
lets the rest of your money stay invested through market ups and downs
instead of being pulled out at the worst possible time.
