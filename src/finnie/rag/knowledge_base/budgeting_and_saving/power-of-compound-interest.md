## Interest earning interest

Simple interest is calculated only on your original amount. Compound
interest is calculated on your original amount *plus* whatever interest
it's already earned -- so your money's growth accelerates over time instead
of staying flat.

## Why starting early matters so much

Because compounding needs time to build momentum, an early start can matter
more than the size of any single contribution. Someone who invests a modest
amount starting in their 20s can end up with more at retirement than someone
who invests a larger amount but starts a decade later -- purely because of
the extra years of compounding, not because they saved more in total.

## A simple illustration

Assume a 7% average annual return (a commonly used simplified assumption
for long-run US stock market history, not a guarantee): $200/month invested
starting at age 25 grows to a meaningfully larger sum by age 65 than the
same $200/month started at age 35, even though the 35-year-old invests for
"only" 10 fewer years. Run the actual numbers with a compound-interest
calculator to see the size of the gap for a given return assumption.

## The other side: compounding debt

The same mechanism works against you with high-interest debt, like credit
cards -- unpaid interest gets added to your balance, and next month's
interest is calculated on that larger balance. This is part of why paying
down high-interest debt is often prioritized ahead of investing.

## Going deeper

Compound growth isn't a straight line in real life -- actual investment
returns vary year to year, sometimes sharply. The "smooth curve" often shown
in illustrations is a simplification of what, in practice, is a bumpy ride
that tends to trend upward over long periods.
