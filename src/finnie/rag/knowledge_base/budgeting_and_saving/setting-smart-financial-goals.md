## Why "save more money" isn't an actionable goal

A vague goal like "save more" or "invest for the future" gives you nothing
concrete to plan around -- no way to know how much to save each month, or
whether you're on track. The SMART framework is a widely used way to turn a
vague intention into something you can actually plan for and measure
progress against.

## The five components

- **Specific**: name the actual goal -- "a house down payment," not just
  "save money."
- **Measurable**: attach a dollar amount -- "$40,000," not "a good amount."
- **Achievable**: realistic given your actual income and expenses, not an
  aspirational number disconnected from your budget.
- **Relevant**: something that genuinely matters to you, not a goal copied
  from somewhere else that doesn't reflect your actual priorities.
- **Time-bound**: a target date -- "in 5 years," not an open-ended "someday."

## Why the target date matters so much for investing

Once a goal has a specific dollar amount and timeline, it directly informs
two practical decisions: how much to contribute each month, and what risk
level is appropriate (a goal five years out generally calls for a more
conservative mix than one thirty years out, as discussed in this
assistant's articles on risk tolerance and goal planning).

## Multiple goals, multiple timelines

Most people are working toward several goals simultaneously with different
timelines -- an emergency fund (immediate), a house down payment
(medium-term), and retirement (long-term). Each can reasonably warrant a
different risk level and a separate way of tracking progress, rather than
lumping all savings into one undifferentiated pool.

## Going deeper

A goal's numbers aren't set in stone -- revisiting and adjusting them
periodically as income, priorities, or circumstances change is a normal
part of the process, not a sign the original goal-setting was done wrong.
