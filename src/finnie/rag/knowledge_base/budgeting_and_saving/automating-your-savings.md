## "Pay yourself first"

A common piece of advice: instead of spending throughout the month and
saving whatever happens to be left over (which, for many people, ends up
being very little), automatically move money toward savings and investing
*before* it has a chance to be spent -- treating saving like a fixed,
non-negotiable bill rather than an afterthought.

## What automation typically looks like in practice

- **Direct payroll contributions** into a 401(k), set up once and running
  in the background from then on.
- **Automatic transfers** from checking to savings on payday.
- **Automatic investment purchases** on a schedule, sometimes called
  dollar-cost averaging when applied to a recurring investment amount
  (covered in more depth elsewhere).

## Why removing the decision helps

Automation reduces the number of times you have to actively choose to
save, which matters because that decision competes with the immediate
pull of everyday spending every single time you'd otherwise have to make
it manually. Behavioral research consistently finds that people save more
reliably when the default action requires no ongoing willpower.

## Setting it up without overcommitting

A common mistake is automating an amount that's unrealistic given monthly
cash flow, leading to overdrafts or constantly pausing the automation.
Starting with a modest, sustainable automatic amount -- and increasing it
gradually, for example each time you get a raise -- tends to stick better
than starting aggressively and abandoning it.

## Going deeper

Automating savings doesn't remove the need to occasionally review your
overall budget and goals -- it's meant to handle the routine, repetitive
part of saving so your attention is freed up for the bigger periodic
decisions, not to be set up once and never revisited.
