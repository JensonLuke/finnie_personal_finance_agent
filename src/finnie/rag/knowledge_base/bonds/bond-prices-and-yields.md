## Two ways to talk about a bond's return

A bond's **coupon rate** is fixed at issuance -- it's the interest rate paid
on the face value and doesn't change. A bond's **yield**, on the other hand,
reflects the return based on its *current market price*, which does change
as the bond trades.

## Why price and yield move in opposite directions

Say a bond pays a fixed $50/year on a $1,000 face value (a 5% coupon). If
newly issued bonds start offering 6% instead, this older 5% bond becomes
less attractive by comparison -- so its market price falls until its yield
(that same $50/year, now relative to a lower price) rises to be competitive
with the new 6% bonds. The reverse happens when rates fall: existing
higher-coupon bonds become more attractive, and their price rises.

## Why this catches beginners off guard

"Bonds are safe" is common advice, but it mostly refers to credit risk (the
issuer paying you back) -- not price stability. A bond fund can lose value
in the short term purely because interest rates rose, even if every bond in
it eventually pays exactly what it promised. This is sometimes called
"interest rate risk."

## Duration as a rough sensitivity measure

Bonds (and bond funds) with longer time until maturity are generally more
sensitive to interest rate changes than short-term bonds -- a concept
covered in more detail in a dedicated article on bond duration. As a rough
rule of thumb, the longer a bond's timeline, the more its price swings when
rates move.

## Going deeper

If you plan to hold an individual bond until it matures, day-to-day price
swings from interest rate changes don't affect what you'll ultimately be
paid at maturity, assuming the issuer doesn't default. That certainty
mostly disappears once you're holding a bond *fund* rather than an
individual bond, since a fund continuously buys and sells bonds and doesn't
have a fixed "maturity" of its own.
