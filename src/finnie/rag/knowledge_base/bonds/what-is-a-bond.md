## Lending, not owning

Buying a stock makes you a part-owner of a company. Buying a bond makes you
a lender. When you buy a bond, you're loaning money to whoever issued it --
a company, a city, or a government -- and they promise to pay you back on a
specific date (the "maturity date"), plus regular interest payments (called
"coupon payments") along the way.

## The main pieces of a bond

- **Face value (par value)**: the amount you'll be paid back at maturity,
  commonly $1,000 per bond.
- **Coupon rate**: the annual interest rate paid on the face value.
- **Maturity date**: when the loan is fully repaid.
- **Issuer**: who's borrowing the money -- this drives how risky the bond is.

## Why bonds are generally considered "safer"

A government or well-established company is usually seen as more likely to
pay back a loan than to keep a stock price rising, so bonds are typically
less volatile than stocks day-to-day. "Safer" doesn't mean "risk-free,"
though -- an issuer can still default (fail to pay), and bond prices do move,
especially when interest rates change.

## Bonds and interest rates move opposite each other

When newly issued bonds start paying a higher interest rate, existing bonds
paying a lower rate become less attractive by comparison, so their market
price tends to fall -- and vice versa. This is one of the most common
sources of confusion for beginners: a "safe" bond fund can still lose value
if rates rise while you're holding it.

## Going deeper

Most beginners don't buy individual bonds directly -- they get bond exposure
through a bond mutual fund or ETF, which holds many bonds at once and
smooths out the ups and downs of any single issuer.
