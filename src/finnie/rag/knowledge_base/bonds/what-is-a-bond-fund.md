## Bonds, pooled together

A bond fund -- whether structured as a mutual fund or an ETF -- holds many
individual bonds at once, letting you get diversified bond exposure in a
single purchase instead of buying individual bonds one at a time (which
often requires larger minimums and more research per bond than most
beginners want to take on).

## What a bond fund smooths out

If a single bond issuer runs into trouble, a diversified bond fund holding
dozens or hundreds of issuers absorbs that impact far better than owning
just that one bond directly would. This is the same diversification
principle that applies to stocks, applied to fixed income.

## The key difference from an individual bond: no fixed maturity

An individual bond has a specific maturity date when you're paid back the
face value. A bond fund doesn't -- it continuously buys new bonds as older
ones mature, so it never "matures" and pays out a lump sum the way a single
bond does. This means a bond fund's value can fluctuate indefinitely with
interest rates, without the "wait until maturity and get your principal
back" resolution an individual bond offers.

## Types of bond funds

Bond funds are commonly categorized by what they hold: government bond
funds, municipal bond funds, corporate bond funds, or "aggregate"/"total
bond market" funds that blend multiple types. They're also categorized by
average duration (short-term, intermediate-term, long-term), which affects
how sensitive the fund is to interest rate changes.

## Going deeper

Checking a bond fund's expense ratio matters just as much as it does for a
stock fund -- fees compound over time and eat into what would otherwise be
modest, steady fixed-income returns even more noticeably than they do for
typically higher-returning stock funds.
