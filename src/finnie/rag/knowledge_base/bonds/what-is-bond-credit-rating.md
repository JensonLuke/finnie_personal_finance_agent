## A shorthand for "how likely is this issuer to pay me back"

Independent rating agencies (the best known being S&P Global Ratings,
Moody's, and Fitch) assess bond issuers -- companies and governments -- and
assign a letter-grade rating meant to summarize their assessed risk of
default (failing to pay back what's owed).

## The rough scale

Ratings generally run from AAA (the highest, considered extremely safe)
down through AA, A, BBB, and then into progressively riskier territory
(BB, B, CCC, and below). The exact letter systems differ slightly between
agencies, but the ordering concept is the same: higher letters mean lower
assessed default risk.

## Investment-grade vs. high-yield ("junk")

Bonds rated BBB-/Baa3 or above are generally called "investment-grade" --
considered relatively safe. Bonds rated below that threshold are called
"high-yield" or, less flatteringly, "junk" bonds. They're not necessarily
bad investments, but they carry meaningfully higher default risk, which is
why they typically have to offer higher interest rates to attract buyers.

## Ratings are an assessment, not a guarantee

Credit ratings are informed opinions based on available information at a
point in time -- they can be wrong, and they can change as an issuer's
financial situation changes. A downgrade (a rating being lowered) often
causes a bond's price to fall, since it signals increased risk to the
market.

## Going deeper

A bond fund's fact sheet usually lists the credit-quality breakdown of its
holdings (e.g. "70% AAA, 20% AA, 10% A"), which is worth checking alongside
duration when comparing bond funds -- two funds that look similar on the
surface can carry very different overall risk profiles.
