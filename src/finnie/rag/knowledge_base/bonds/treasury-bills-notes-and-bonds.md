US government debt is split into a few categories based purely on how long
until it matures -- the terms get used almost interchangeably in casual
conversation, but they refer to specific maturity ranges.

## Treasury Bills ("T-Bills")

Maturities of one year or less. T-Bills don't pay a separate interest
coupon -- instead, they're sold at a discount to face value, and you're paid
the full face value at maturity; the difference is your return. Their short
timeline makes them among the least sensitive Treasury securities to
interest rate changes.

## Treasury Notes ("T-Notes")

Maturities of 2 to 10 years. Unlike bills, notes pay a fixed coupon every
six months, plus the face value back at maturity.

## Treasury Bonds ("T-Bonds")

Maturities of 20 or 30 years -- the longest-dated regular Treasury
securities. Like notes, they pay a fixed coupon every six months. Being
longest-dated, they carry the most interest-rate sensitivity of the three.

## Why these matter beyond direct ownership

Treasury yields, especially the widely watched 10-year Treasury Note, act
as a benchmark that ripples through the rest of the financial system --
mortgage rates, corporate bond yields, and many other borrowing costs move
in relation to them. You don't need to own Treasuries directly to be
affected by what happens to their yields.

## Going deeper

There's also Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS), whose
principal adjusts with inflation -- a distinct category from bills, notes,
and bonds, aimed specifically at protecting purchasing power rather than
just providing a fixed return.
