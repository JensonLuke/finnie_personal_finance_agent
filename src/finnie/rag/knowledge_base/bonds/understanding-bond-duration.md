## Not the same as time to maturity

It's tempting to think "duration" just means "years until the bond
matures," but in finance it's a more specific measure: roughly, how
sensitive a bond's price is to a change in interest rates, expressed in
years. A bond with a duration of 7 means its price would be expected to
move about 7% for each 1-percentage-point change in interest rates
(approximately, and in the opposite direction of the rate change).

## Why it's not identical to maturity

A bond that pays a lot of interest along the way (a high coupon) returns
more of your money to you sooner than a bond that pays little interest and
mostly repays value at maturity -- so, all else equal, a higher-coupon bond
has a *shorter* duration than an otherwise-similar lower-coupon bond with
the same maturity date, because more of its value is "returned" earlier.

## Practical takeaway for fund shoppers

Bond fund fact sheets usually publish an average duration figure. A fund
with a duration of 2 is far less sensitive to interest rate moves than one
with a duration of 15 -- useful context when comparing two bond funds that
might otherwise look similar (both "investment grade," say) but carry very
different levels of interest-rate risk.

## Matching duration to your timeline

A common rule of thumb: if you'll need money in the near future, shorter
duration reduces the chance that a poorly-timed rate move dents your
balance right when you need it. Longer-duration bonds are more often used
by investors with a longer time horizon, who can wait out a temporary price
decline.

## Going deeper

Duration is a linear approximation and becomes less accurate for very large
interest rate moves -- a more precise (and more complex) measure called
"convexity" accounts for that curvature, but duration alone is normally
good enough for a beginner comparing fund options.
